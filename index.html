“Lessons” Learnt

Clients anonymised

“Challenging a decision already made lands when you bring evidence and a clear walkthrough.”

Method — Design review, code review, penetration test
The situation
The platform had been redesigned into decoupled UI, authorisation and payment services, and one component had never been tested in that form. An earlier vendor had cleared the pre-redesign version a year before. Go-live was close and leadership was watching.
What I did
I argued to keep the full scope and offered weekend shifts to fit it. I read design documents, earlier threat models and codebases beyond scope, then found and exploited a chain of four flaws, and walked the Payments SVP and AppSec VPs through it with live demonstrations.
Outcome
The client accepted all four. The finding was a major part of the case for the mid-2025 contract renewal, alongside the team’s other work.
Further reading
■The chain: an XSS through a legacy UI service that was never decommissioned; insecure state validation in the subscriber-push configuration; loosely coupled service calls with a confused-deputy pattern; and a bypass that let immutable payment details be edited.
■The XSS was the entry point. The other three turned it into business-logic impact in the payment workflows, with real financial consequences, including insider-threat scenarios.
■Stakeholders pushed back for two reasons: the components had been cleared, and go-live was close. The earlier vendor had tested the coupled version, not the decoupled one.
■I had tested and exploited the chain before the walkthrough; live demonstrations to the SVP, AppSec VPs, product owners and developers turned the pushback into acceptance.
■The payment systems ran as microservices on AWS; my design reviews covered the split of responsibility between provider and client.
■The renewal was a team and account result my work contributed to. The bank later chose to consolidate its delivery in Bangalore.

“An AI tool is only as trustworthy as the evidence you check it against, so the checking step is the methodology.”

Method — STRIDE, OWASP Risk Rating, AWS Threat Designer, LLM validation
The situation
The threat modelling tool invented scope, duplicated data flows and misjudged likelihood and severity. Bangalore didn’t offer threat modelling at all, and new assessors needed one method to work from.
What I did
I proposed the service, delivered the first assessments with a method I built, then built a workflow around the tool: retrieval over the practice’s pages primes its input, and a separate model checks each claim against the client’s documents and code.
Outcome
A comparable assessment took one week instead of three. I was appointed practice lead and assessors are now onboarding with the workflow.
Further reading
■What the tool got wrong: redundant data flows, assets and components; invented and out-of-scope components; claims that conflicted with the evidence; inaccurate likelihood, impact and severity.
■Before an assessment the workflow turns documents, codebases, customer expectations and methodology into the structured input the tool consumes. Assessors also use it to look up or learn threat modelling.
■After the tool runs, separate, isolated model contexts validate each claim, with a prompt per case: scope, attack surfaces, threat agents, components, remediation and existing controls.
■The client signs off on the tooling, including this workflow and the use of client data in it.
■It started with a gap I saw while my other work ran smoothly. Making it official needed director sign-off, the client manager, a sales offering and moving customers to the India team; I was appointed only after the first assessments succeeded.
■I also provide technical oversight on the practice’s assessments, owning each one’s scope and reviewing its deliverables, without line authority over the assessors.

“For AI systems, the question shifts from “who can reach this?” to “what can this agent do on someone’s behalf?””

Method — STRIDE × OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications
The situation
An enterprise GenAI platform on AWS (an LLM gateway, an agent configuration platform and an MCP-enabled support chatbot) was heading for launch, with no established playbook for agentic AI.
What I did
I mapped trust boundaries, applied the OWASP LLM list with STRIDE and assessed guardrails. In a design walkthrough I found that a token meant only to configure agents also opened the central gateway, so support tooling could be used to extract passenger information.
Outcome
The client’s developers confirmed it exploitable, and the findings set the scope of the follow-on penetration test. The first of the three was the first threat model delivered from Bangalore.
Further reading
■Biggest risks: excessive agency, over-permissioned agents and MCP tools, supply chain, retrieval and embedding weaknesses, and unbounded consumption.
■The chain: SSO, then the token, then the LLM gateway, then the agent service layer, then the GPT service layer.
■Because it was a design flaw, every agent and tool built on the platform was susceptible: an enterprise-wide attack surface.
■I found it in a design walkthrough, not by live testing. The client’s developers tested it internally and confirmed it exploitable.
■The client owns the fix; revalidation hasn’t been requested yet, so it isn’t verified.
■I broke complex requirements into caveats and edge cases and mapped upstream and downstream impact so each stakeholder saw what a finding meant for them. Each assessment was peer reviewed by AppRisk leadership.

“Self-sufficiency doesn’t come from process alone; people have to be shown how visibility and accountability work.”

Method — Deliverable review, coaching, shared knowledge base
The situation
A team of 8 assessors worked reactively, because their process had never built the ownership habits I’d seen succeed on the bank account. Report revisions and escalations were recurring problems.
What I did
I reviewed every deliverable for accuracy and severity, unblocked assessors mid-engagement and taught them to keep runbooks, learn the client’s trackers, log effort against the SLA and treat stakeholder communication as a skill.
Outcome
Assessors led client calls unsupervised, escalations and report revisions dropped to zero, and the contract expanded.
Further reading
■I taught that ownership leads to accountability, which shows in how performance is judged.
■Visibility comes from initiative: keep your own runbooks, understand the trackers your technical leads and managers keep, and use enumeration and pivoting on the work around the job: processes, client expectations, escalations, root causes.
■Writing VAPT reports became practice for writing escalation replies; logging effort against what was delivered made the work visible.
■The numbers come from the project managers’ contract tracker, presented at renewal. My team stayed on web and API; mobile testing was added through another team.
■The team ran smoothly enough to free my time to start the threat modelling practice alongside.

“Automate the repeatable checks so people can spend their time on the judgement calls.”

Method — Python, vulnerability management data
The situation
Vulnerability management data, bug bounty reports and past penetration test findings sat in separate places, so revalidating old and sibling findings was slow and repetitive.
What I did
I wrote Python automation that pulls vulnerable requests and payloads from the vulnerability management portals into one attack-surface view, adds bug bounty data and old reports, and auto-runs known exploits against later versions, sibling codebases and shared services.
Outcome
A day of work became half a day, developers got answers faster, and assessors spent the saved time on deeper business-logic testing. Other assessors adopted the script.
Further reading
■Before the script, revalidation was manual: check the risk portals, validate old findings and bug bounty reports, and repeat on sibling codebases.
■I built it alone between February 2024 and July 2025.
■The 50% is my own timing: one day before, half a day after.

“Problem-solving isn’t tied to job title. The most useful thing I did that year wasn’t a finding.”

Method — Runbooks, guidebooks, feedback loops
The situation
The bank was comparing consultancies and we were on a trial. The team lead was stretched, and assessors and project managers each read the contract, SLAs and client expectations differently.
What I did
I became the first point of contact for escalations alongside my own assessments, listed the pain points, and built a training, lookup and feedback resource of runbooks and guidebooks on the client’s specific practices.
Outcome
Assessors and project managers became self-sufficient, the lead’s time freed up, and other delivery teams for the account adopted it. It was part of the team effort that moved the client from a trial to an annual contract and earned the ACE Award for Customer Focus.
Further reading
■The bank’s systems were multi-tenant, federated platforms of many services, so the hard part was tracking systemic findings across sibling codebases and shared services.
■Upstream and downstream dependencies, publish-subscribe models and dual-approval controls widened every revalidation, which surfaced perimeter-only security, security by obscurity, distributed sensitive-data exposure and second-order attacks.
■My team adopted the methodology in 2022 and other teams in 2024. Pooling everyone’s output meant findings, risks and expectations could be tracked and communicated at scale.
■The trial-to-contract result and the award were team results my work contributed to.

“Speed and quality aren’t opposites if you’re methodical about where you spend the time.”

Method — White-box shortcuts, Python proofs of concept
The situation
An assessor went on leave after submitting incomplete work on the SLA date. Other vendors had already reported serious issues in the same authentication module, our logs were missing test cases, and the client escalated and shortened the deadline.
What I did
Black-box testing wouldn’t fit the time, so I borrowed from white-box methods: scan logs, tickets, documentation and automated scan reports showed which workflows mattered. I confirmed missing authentication and a blind SSRF, scripted both proofs in Python and wrote the business-impact scenarios.
Outcome
The client’s escalation turned into praise for the speed and quality, and the company director recognised the work.
Further reading
■I updated the client and internal contacts at every step.
■A different client from the bank: a global enterprise IT modernisation software vendor.

“My responsibility isn’t only to clients but to the people I work with. Showing how works better than telling.”

Method — Shadowing, guided exercises
The situation
A skilled but demoralised junior colleague had hit a wall trying to get remote code execution in a hardened environment.
What I did
I asked management to let him shadow me and talked through my thinking, resources and what mattered to the client. I showed him how, because of how the framework behaved, the same vector could give SSRF instead, then set him a follow-up task: build a small subnet scanner to see the real impact.
Outcome
He said it rebuilt his confidence, and later found business-logic issues on his own for an important client, crediting that exercise.
Further reading
■I mentored several junior assessors the same way during 2021 to 2024, on attack-chain thinking and client communication.

“Doing right by the client and keeping them happy can conflict. I make my case with evidence, then commit once the decision is made.”

Method — Evidence, hands-on walkthrough
The situation
I rated a flaw high in a critical admin application that had never been tested. The client’s security team asked me to lower it, not because they doubted the risk but because a high rating meant extra work for them.
What I did
I raised it with my technical lead, then ran a hands-on walkthrough of the impact with the client’s own developer, who agreed it was real. When asked, I recommended keeping the rating.
Outcome
The final decision was a compromise in favour of the client relationship. It wasn’t what I argued for, but the walkthrough meant the client understood the real risk.
Further reading
■From experience with this client, I knew a lower rating meant it wouldn’t be fixed soon.

“Taking initiative is good, but I now clarify roles and constraints before committing to parallel work, and flag risks as soon as I see them.”

Method — Early escalation
The situation
Early on, a project manager asked if I could run two assessments at once. I said yes without checking how the workload would be managed, carrying over an assumption from other accounts.
What I did
Partway through I realised I would meet both client deadlines but miss the internal review. I told my technical lead early rather than at the last minute, and he moved the review window.
Outcome
I missed the internal review deadline, but because I flagged it early it stayed an internal scheduling issue, not a client one.
